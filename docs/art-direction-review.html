Art direction review Claw Machine Cuties · v1 · single-file build

The art is good.
The staging is what fails.

Forty-eight hand-drawn plushies, six cabinets, an honest physics grab — and inside the machine you can't tell one plushie from another. That is not a drawing problem. Every character in this game already reads beautifully somewhere in the build: open the album and they're charming. Put the same art in the pit and it turns to mush.

This review works through what changed between those two screens, and what a small number of edits to the art layer would do for both the look and the aim. Ten plates: diagnosis, a character system, all forty-eight cuties, the pit, the six worlds, the reward moments, and a build order.

Plate 01

What's actually wrong

One root cause, three symptoms, and a control experiment already sitting in your build.

Root cause

Every plushie is drawn as flat fills with no outline and no shadow, then placed on a pale tinted ground of almost exactly the same lightness. There is nothing — no keyline, no cast shadow, no value break — separating a character from the wall behind it or from the character next to it. In the teddy machine you get a brown blur. In the unicorn machine, where sixteen pastel bodies overlap on pastel pink, you get an unreadable carpet.

The second half of the same cause: variants are differentiated by hue alone. Eight teddies, one silhouette, one pose, one bow placement — only the fur colour and the bow colour change. Trace any of them in black and you get the same shape eight times. At the size a plushie occupies in the pit — roughly 60 px on a phone — hue is the weakest signal you have, and it is the only one you're using.

Why it matters

This isn't only a beauty problem, and that's what makes it the top priority. Your README states the game's whole skill expression: "aim — how close to a plushie's centre the claw came down" and "exposure — how many other plushies are sitting on top of the one you grabbed." Both ask the player to identify an individual target and judge whether it is buried. The art currently makes both judgements impossible. The grab maths is scrupulously fair; the picture doesn't let the player play it.

The control

The album screen is the proof. Same art functions, same palettes, same characters — but each one sits alone on a white card with space around it, and every plushie is instantly legible and genuinely appealing. You do not need new characters. You need the pit to treat them the way the album does.

Plate 03

The target style

Your two reference sheets — the cow-of-the-month set and the fruit-costume animals — agree on five techniques. All five are things the current art doesn't do, and all five are cheap in SVG.

Extracted
  • 01A die-cut keyline. Every character in both references carries a thick cream-white outline, like a printed sticker. It is the single reason those characters stay legible against any ground — and the single biggest thing missing here.
  • 02Soft interior volume. Not flat fills: a warm shadow gathers under the chin, between the limbs and around the lower body, with a soft highlight top-left. The characters read as stuffed, not printed.
  • 03Costumes carry identity, not colour — and they change the outline. The cow sheet is one cow twelve times, but look at what actually varies: one has wings, one is a mushroom, one is buried in flowers, one is a strawberry. It is not a hat swap. The fruit-animal sheet does the same — a watermelon hood, a leaf collar, an orange cap that reshapes the head. The rule is: if you traced the character in black, could you still tell which one it is? That's the bar, and it's the bar your hue-only variants fail.
  • 04A muted palette on a neutral ground. Both sheets sit on cream or white, and the character colours are desaturated. Contrast lives between character and ground, not inside the character. Your build inverts this: saturated ground, pale characters.
  • 05Garnish, scattered loosely. Petals, seeds, crumbs and sparkles sit around the feet, outside the keyline. Cheap, and it's what makes a sticker sheet feel hand-made rather than generated.
Note

Rule 03 resolves a tension worth naming, because the obvious reading of each half is wrong. From the screenshots alone the call is "draw eight different animals" — which loses the set. From the references alone the call is "one body, eight hats" — which is too timid to fix anything at 60 px. The references are actually doing something more specific: one base body, costumed hard enough to change the outline. Keep the bear underneath. Then put it inside a pumpkin, under a mushroom cap, behind a pair of wings.

Plate 04

The character system

Everything below renders live from your own artTeddy() palettes. The proposed row is the same eight colourways — nothing recoloured, nothing redrawn from scratch.

Specimen A  The keyline, on the pit's own ground

The background here is the exact gradient behind the plushies in Teddy Bear Bonanza. Current: the pale bears dissolve into it. Proposed: a 2.2 px cream die-cut, a warm radial shade pass, a top-left highlight and a contact shadow. Same colours, same ground, same eight characters.

How

A rounded die-cut is a blur of the alpha channel pushed through a steep transfer function. feMorphology is the obvious tool and it's the wrong one — its structuring element is rectangular, so it squares off ears and paws. Define this once per document and every plushie, in the pit and the album alike, inherits it.

<filter id="diecut" x="-40%" y="-40%" width="180%" height="180%">
  <!-- blur the silhouette, then threshold it back to a hard edge -->
  <feGaussianBlur in="SourceAlpha" stdDeviation="2.2" result="b"/>
  <feComponentTransfer in="b" result="t">
    <feFuncA type="linear" slope="18" intercept="-1.4"/>
  </feComponentTransfer>
  <feFlood flood-color="#fffaf3" result="c"/>
  <feComposite in="c" in2="t" operator="in" result="ol"/>
  <feMerge><feMergeNode in="ol"/><feMergeNode in="SourceGraphic"/></feMerge>
</filter>

Then in paintBody(), wrap the art: <g filter="url(#diecut)">…</g>. That's the whole change, and it is the highest-leverage edit in this document.

Specimen B  The mild tier: props and accessories

One base bear, six accessories: strawberry, mushroom, flower crown, leaf collar, oversized bow, crown. Squint at this row, then squint at the row above — the props survive the squint and the hues don't. This is already a large improvement on a recolour, and it is not far enough. Keep going to Specimen C.

Specimen C  Costumes: identity that survives being traced in black

The same base bear seven times, on the pit's own ground: plain, winged, pumpkin-bodied, mushroom-capped, flower-covered, watermelon-hooded, crowned. Every one of these has a different outline. Cover the colour and you can still name them — that's the test a hue swap can never pass, and it's what makes a machine full of these read as a pile of individuals instead of a texture.

Three tiers

Not every variant needs the full treatment — a roster where all eight are radical reads as noise. Mix the tiers, weight the rare ones higher, and give every machine at least three at tier S.

Specimen D  The rarity ladder

Common — cream keyline, no prop. Rare — cream keyline plus a signature prop. Legendary — a gold halo outside the keyline, a crown, and four sparkles that sit beyond the die-cut. Your data model already carries rare: 0 | 1 | 2 with drop weights of 1 / .42 / .15, and the art expresses none of it — a legendary currently arrives looking exactly like a common, with the word legendary printed under it. This ladder costs one branch in stuffyArt().

Proportion

One shape note carried over from the references, applied in the specimens above. The current bear's head (r 28) and body (ry 21) are near enough the same size that it reads as an adult figure. Both reference sheets run a much harder chibi ratio: a head roughly 1.7× the body, stubby limbs, and facial features clustered small and low on the face. The proposed bear moves the head to r 30, the body to ry 17, and drops the eyes and muzzle into the lower third. It is a friendlier read at every size, and it gains you silhouette headroom for the props to sit in.

Plate 05

All forty-eight cuties

A costume and a one-line identity for every character, built from the name you already gave it. Nothing is renamed and no palette is thrown away — but a good half of these change shape, because half is what it takes.

Reading the tables. A is an accessory, C is a costume, S is a silhouette change — the tiers from Plate 04. Each roster is weighted so the rares and the legendary sit at S: the harder something is to win, the more distinct it should look sitting in the pile you're trying to dig it out of.

Plate 06

The pit

Level design, singular — six machines currently share one interior. This is where the game is played and where the most is being left on the table.

Specimen E  Interior composition

Both panels use the game's real cabinet geometry and its real projection — sOf(z), projX, projY — so the proposal is achievable inside the existing physics rather than a repaint over it.

Five changes
  • 01Fill the box. Plushies rest at y = r × 0.58 and never stack, so they occupy a band roughly 85 px tall inside a 400 px glass — about a fifth. Meanwhile the claw's rest height leaves a 200 px column of empty tinted air. Raise the pit floor and let the pile stack to 50–60% of the box. This is what makes a claw machine look full of prizes, and it gives the claw somewhere to dive.
  • 02Neutralise the interior. Back wall and floor become warm cream, not a saturated tint of the machine's colour. The cabinet keeps the colour; the interior is a lightbox. This is reference rule 04, and it's what lets the characters do the work.
  • 03A contact shadow under every plushie. One ellipse, scaled and faded by height. Your README says depth is half the aim — but nothing on screen currently tells the player where anything sits in z. This single ellipse turns an unreadable overlap into legible depth, and it makes the exposure term of your grab score visible for the first time.
  • 04An aim pool under the claw. A dashed ring on the floor at the claw's (x, z), occluded by anything in front of it. The player is being scored on centring accuracy against a target they cannot currently see. This isn't an assist — it shows what the machine already knows.
  • 05Light the chute. Right now the prize hole is a dark ellipse behind the pile, and its PRIZE label is drawn at y ≈ 514 — underneath the 15 px glass frame that ends at 516, so it's sliced in half on every machine. Move it to the front-left corner, give it a gold rim, and let the pile never cover it. It's the goal; it should be the second-brightest thing in the box.
Cut

Delete the GRAB ME! sign. It's a plaque on the back wall in the exact centre of the glass, and it exists to fill the emptiness identified in change 01. Once the pit is full it has nowhere to go, and it currently reads as a UI label floating in the play space. The pile is the invitation.

Plate 07

Six machines, six worlds

Right now the six cabinets are one cabinet rendered in six hues — same marquee, same bulbs, same polka dots, same back wall, same tagline plate. Difficulty climbs steeply from 0.62 to 0.19 grip and nothing in the picture warns you.

Two machines make the problem concrete. Teddy Bear Bonanza is pink on a pink arcade backdrop and Unicorn Dream Machine is also pink — the first and last machines, the easiest and the hardest, are the two least distinguishable objects in the lobby. Give each machine an interior that its plushies live in, and let the interior carry the difficulty tell.

The big one

Make the unicorn machine dark. It is the only change in this document that alters the arcade's overall colour rhythm, and it earns three things at once: the finale finally looks like a finale; sixteen pale pastel plushies become the brightest things on screen instead of the most camouflaged; and after five bright cabinets, walking into a dark one lands. Everything else in the arcade stays candy-bright — spend the boldness here.

Lobby

Two smaller notes on the arcade screen. The cards float in a tall gradient void with the floor perspective barely visible at the bottom — set them on the floor. Give each card a cast shadow onto the perspective plane and pull the floor line up so the lobby reads as a room you're standing in rather than a grid on a wallpaper. And the machine thumbnails currently show three plushies behind glass at 0.30 scale, which at card size is three coloured smudges; show one hero plushie large, the machine's own legendary, and let the card be a poster for it.

Plate 08

The reward moments

A grab lands about a third of the time at best. The moments that pay that off are carrying a lot of weight and two of the three aren't carrying it.

Win modal

Stage the reveal instead of printing it. The prize wants a pedestal and a light: a soft radial pool behind the plushie, rays for a rare, a rotating gold sunburst for a legendary — the rarity ladder from Plate 04, at full size, where the player is finally looking straight at it. Add the one piece of information the screen is missing: NEW versus ×3, so a duplicate reads as a duplicate before the player reaches the album. And the key currently floats over the bear's arm rather than being held — tuck it into a paw.

Parade

Three fixes, in order of payoff. Dedupe and interleave — one float per kind, with a ×3 flag on the float's nameplate, so the parade is a tour of the collection rather than a production line. Fill the sky — bring the road up to about 60% of the viewport, put two or three float lanes at different scales scrolling at different speeds, and hang the bunting properly across the top rather than a single thin swag. Hold the Continue button back — it currently sits above the parade before a float has arrived, inviting the player to dismiss the celebration they just earned three grabs for; fade it in after the first pass.

One addition worth its weight: the plushies you just won lead the parade. Put the newest three at the front, largest, with the key-holder carrying the key. The parade is the only screen in the game that could show a player their own run, and right now it shows them roster order.

Album

Nearly right, and two changes would finish it. The empty slots all show the same generic per-machine silhouette, so an uncollected bear is indistinguishable from another uncollected bear — silhouette the actual character, prop included, and a missing Bubblegum bunny becomes a bunny-with-a-bubble-shaped hole that a player can go hunting for. Second, separate the reset button from Close. A destructive, unrecoverable action currently sits directly beside the dismiss button, in the same size and shape.

And the album is where the collection lives — give it a shelf. The grid is efficient but flat; a wooden shelf line under each row, with the plushies sitting on it and casting the same contact shadow they cast in the pit, turns a data table into a bedroom shelf.

Also

Pick one art language. The marquee sets 🧸 emoji beside your own hand-drawn bears; the backdrop, the confetti and the progress dots are all system emoji too. The emoji render differently on every platform, they don't match the characters they sit next to, and they undercut the single most impressive thing about this build — that every pixel of it is drawn in code. You already have the vocabulary to replace them: the plushies themselves, sparkleArt(), bowArt() and keyArt(). Swap the confetti for small drawn hearts, bows and stars, and put a drawn hero plushie in the marquee instead of an emoji.

Closing

Steps 1 through 4 are an afternoon, and they are most of the difference between the pit and the album.

The rest is a costume department. Nothing in this review asks you to throw away a character, a palette, a name or a line of physics — the grab maths is honest and it should stay exactly as it is. What it asks is that the picture finally show the player what the maths already knows: which plushie is which, how deep it sits, what's on top of it, and which one is worth going after.
